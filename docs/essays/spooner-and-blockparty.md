# Lysander Spooner and BlockParty

Lysander Spooner, the 19th-century radical individualist and legal theorist, argued that all legitimate governance must derive from explicit, voluntary consent. To Spooner, the so-called "social contract" offered by states was a fraud—a coercive illusion maintained by historical inertia and brute force. No contract, he asserted, could be binding unless every participant personally and continuously agreed to its terms.

BlockParty operationalizes the world Spooner only envisioned. It creates a technological substrate where association, memory, and identity are no longer permissions granted by authorities but voluntary acts proven through cryptographic consent.

## Consent Beyond Rhetoric

Spooner was unflinching: if an individual did not personally sign and agree to a contract, they were bound by nothing. Governments that claimed otherwise were tyrannies masquerading as mutual agreements.

BlockParty embodies this standard technologically. Every block is signed. Every association is voluntary. Worlds, audiences, and memories form, persist, and diverge solely through ongoing consent—not historical claims or inherited obligations.

There is no "binding citizenship" in BlockParty. No inherited identity. No unavoidable registry. Association is a living act, renewed at every interaction.

## Memory Without Sovereign Captors

Spooner recognized that monopolies on communication—like state control of postal services—were extensions of illegitimate force. Memory, too, when monopolized, becomes a tool of domination.

BlockParty refuses to monopolize memory. It treats memory as a sovereign act: you remember what you choose to carry. Histories are forkable. Divergences are legitimate. There is no enforced consensus, only living consensus, built and rebuilt through voluntary action.

If no one chooses to remember a block, it fades. If many choose to carry it, it lives. No central authority dictates what persists. Only free individuals do.

## Identity as Living Will

For Spooner, identity was a matter of voluntary association and contract—not a bureaucratic category assigned by a state.

In BlockParty, identity is portable, revocable, and self-owned. There is no registrar, no permissioned gate. You can exist, vanish, fork, or recreate your presence at will. The cryptographic primitives merely prove action—they do not define the human behind the signature.

Your history is yours to carry—or to burn.

## Conclusion

Spooner challenged the world to imagine a society where no one could be bound without their living consent. BlockParty answers that challenge.

It does not require force to protect speech. It does not require oaths to preserve memory. It does not require allegiance to anchor identity.

It offers a neutral architecture where consent is not a permission to be granted—but a permanent right to be exercised.

Spooner fought for a world where no man could be ruled without choosing it anew every day. BlockParty provides the infrastructure where that principle can live—block by block, world by world, act by sovereign act.
