# Why We Built BlockParty

In 2015, the internet was shifting. Platforms were consolidating, speech was becoming increasingly policed, and entire lives were being dismantled over comments made a decade earlier on MySpace or early Facebook. Some of those comments were foolish, even offensive. But they weren’t crimes. They were moments. Expressions. And the consequences were disproportionate, often devastating.

Around that same time, new ideas were emerging. Mastodon launched and caught attention with its federated model. It was interesting. Fun, even. But it wasn’t trying to reimagine the core assumptions of social networks. It was a platform with more nodes, not a protocol with more principles.

I wanted something else. I didn’t want a server, or a brand, or an algorithm. I didn’t want blockchain-based social graphs with permanent identities and immutable posts. I didn’t want a world where a teenager’s vent or a young adult’s angry joke could be resurfaced and weaponized a decade later.

What I wanted was something humane. Something safe enough that I could imagine my kids using it. Not sanitized or sanitized-by-default. But something that respected growth. Something where they could be loud, silly, stupid, curious, emotional — and not have it become a record that binds them forever.

I wanted the signal to be unstoppable — not in the sense of going viral, but in the sense of being *uncensorable*. I wanted people to be able to speak without permission, to connect without exposure, and to walk away without being tracked.

I’ve always held strong convictions against censorship — not just when it suits my views, but always. Regardless of country, side, or issue. I believe people should be able to say what they need to say. And I believe other people should be able to ignore it, block it, disagree with it — but not erase it from existence or punish the speaker forever.

That’s why BlockParty came to be. It wasn’t born out of trend-chasing or techno-idealism. It came from a simple question:

**What would a social network look like if it were designed with dignity in mind?**

Over the years, the protocol evolved. I prototyped different parts. I paused more than once — even questioning whether such a tool could be dangerous in the wrong hands. But the truth is: the dangerous hands already have tools. The solution isn’t to stop building. It’s to build with care, to release it responsibly, and to give people the option to communicate in a way that doesn’t require them to sell themselves to do it.

BlockParty is not a platform. It’s not a product. It’s a protocol. One that can run in high latency, low bandwidth, or no-network environments. One that lets people post, connect, transact, and disappear. One that supports commerce, community, and mutability — without making permanence the price of participation.

There’s no business model. No launch event. No pitch deck. Just a protocol I built because the ones we had didn’t go far enough. A protocol I’ll be proud for my kids to use.

And if someone else picks it up and carries it further — I’ll be proud of that too.
